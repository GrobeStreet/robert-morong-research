On verifying AI claims before they set a price

The 0.33‑point moat

A target’s “18-point AI advantage,” taken apart. What survived contact with a new customer was almost nothing — and standard diligence never would have caught it.

By Robert Morong· Independent claim verification· ~9 min read

A private-equity firm once bought an AI fraud-detection company at four times revenue. The thesis rested on a single represented number: 98% detection accuracy. It was a real number. It had been produced by real code on a real test set. And it was wrong about the only thing that mattered.

The model had been evaluated on a curated set that did not look like the fraud it would meet in production. On live traffic, accuracy fell to about 71%. The premium — the part of the price that existed only because of the AI story — was gone inside the first year of the hold. Nobody had lied. The number was true in the seller’s harness and false everywhere else, and no line in a standard technical-diligence report is built to tell those two states apart.

I spend my time on that gap. What follows is a teardown of a claim — how a represented advantage comes apart when you actually re-run it — worked on a deliberately constructed target so I can show every number. The target is invented. The method is not; the same method runs publicly, today, on research claims, and I’ll show you that at the end.

01Why the number lies

Private equity is buying AI at record rates and paying premiums for “proprietary” models and “data moats.” Meanwhile the diligence playbook underneath those deals was built for a different kind of asset. Traditional technical diligence is very good at what it was designed for: reading a static codebase, confirming historical recurring revenue, checking that the cloud architecture is sane. None of that is equipped to answer the question an AI premium actually depends on — does the claimed performance survive outside the conditions the seller chose?

A model’s headline metric is a measurement, and every measurement carries the fingerprints of how it was taken. Change the comparator, the customer mix, or the slice of time, and the number moves. Sellers are not usually committing fraud; they are, entirely rationally, reporting the most favorable true measurement available. The buyer’s job — and it is a job almost no one is staffed to do — is to re-take the measurement under conditions the seller didn’t get to pick.

Reproducing a number and verifying a claim are different skills. Most diligence does the first and calls it the second.

02Four tests

Independent verification of a technical claim comes down to four questions, asked in order. Each one is a place a represented advantage tends to leak.

Real. Does it reproduce outside the seller’s environment? Rebuild the claim from the evidence in the room and run it yourself. A result that only holds inside the vendor’s own harness is a marketing artifact, not an asset.

Proprietary. Is the advantage the target’s architecture, or a handicapped comparator? A large share of most “we beat the field by X” claims is a weak or misconfigured baseline on the other side of the comparison. Normalize it and a lot of the gap simply evaporates.

Durable. Does it survive transfer? Re-measure on fresh customers, shifted distributions, and later time periods. An edge that lives only inside the cohort the model was tuned on is not a moat you can underwrite for a five-year hold.

Economic. Narrow or fully loaded? Bridge the unit economics from the story to reality — inference cost, retention, comparator effects, key-person risk — and see whether the margin thesis is still standing at the end.

03The teardown

Here is what those four questions do to a real-looking claim. Call the target Meridian — a vertical AI-SaaS business. Management represents a +18.375-point performance advantage over the field, attributed to “proprietary AI and a defensible data moat,” and that advantage is doing the heavy lifting in the ask. We reconstruct it from the data room and take it apart.

Read the two bars against each other and the whole thesis reorganizes. The Proprietary test finds that more than half the represented advantage — 9.75 of 18.375 points — was a comparator artifact: the target had benchmarked against a baseline that was, for one reason or another, running with a hand tied behind its back. That is not cheating; it is the single most common way a technical claim inflates. It is also invisible unless someone rebuilds and re-runs the comparison.

The Real and architecture piece holds up: 5.125 points come from genuine engineering. But genuine is not the same as defensible. If a competent team could rebuild it in two quarters, it is a feature, not a moat, and it should be priced as one.

Which leaves the data moat — the 3.5 points that are supposed to justify the AI premium. The Durable test is where it dies. Measured on customers outside the cohort the model was shaped on, the data contribution collapses from 3.5 points to 0.33. The advantage the buyer is paying a premium to own is, on the customers the buyer will actually acquire, roughly one-third of one point. The moat is priced at something like fifty-five times what survives contact with a new customer.

None of this says don’t buy Meridian. It says buy it for what is actually there — a decent product with replicable engineering — and not for a data moat that isn’t. The number that changes is the price, and the confidence behind it.

04Why this keeps happening

The incentives are stable and they all point the same way. Sellers are rewarded for producing the best true number inside a harness they control. Advisors are rewarded for closing. The buyer’s technical diligence, however diligent, is usually scoped to confirm that the software exists and works — not to adversarially re-measure a model’s claimed edge under conditions the seller would not have chosen. And the AI premium, the part most exposed to all of this, is precisely the part with the least evidence underneath it.

The fix is not more slides or a longer questionnaire. It is independent reproduction: someone outside the deal who rebuilds the claim, normalizes the comparison, and tests whether the edge transfers — and who leaves a trail you can check. Everything in the Meridian teardown was reconstructed from data-room evidence plus a re-run comparator, and every number traces back to its source. That trail is the actual deliverable. The verdict is just the headline on top of it.

05What a buyer should demand

If you underwrite AI or software and you take one thing from this, take the list. Before an AI premium reaches your investment committee, require that someone has:

The five things to re-run before you pay for a moat

  1. Reproduced the headline number outside the vendor’s harness. If it only runs where the seller runs it, it isn’t evidence.
  2. Normalized the comparator. Rebuild the baseline the target measured itself against, configured fairly. Watch how much of the “advantage” survives.
  3. Tested transfer on fresh customers. Re-measure on accounts outside the design cohort and on later time periods. A moat that needs its home cohort is not a moat.
  4. Bridged narrow to fully-loaded economics. Carry the margin story through inference cost, retention, and key-person and comparator effects to the number you’d actually earn.
  5. Left provenance. Every conclusion should trace to a source you can open. If you can’t audit the verdict, it isn’t one.

06The method is public

It is fair to ask whether this is just a confident narrator. So the same verification method runs in the open, on a harder and fully checkable domain: research claims.

RCV-Bench · public & reproducible

RCV-Bench is a benchmark for exactly this discipline. You hand an agent a claimed numeric result and its code, and it must return a verdict — reproduced, deviation, fabricated, or robust-turned-fragile — with a deterministic, source-traceable trail, scored on real public research repositories, rebuilt by continuous integration on every commit.
